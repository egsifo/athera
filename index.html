Problem

The problem we’re solving is that astronauts on long missions, such as trips to the Moon and Mars, are far from Earth and from the medical team that normally monitors their health in real time. Communication with Earth is delayed by 3 to 22 minutes each way and can drop out completely, so in an emergency there is no time to wait for a reply. At the same time, their bodies face several risks at once: radiation, microgravity that affects the heart and muscles, and isolation that disrupts sleep and mental well-being. These risks build up gradually, and the signals that reveal them, like heart rate and blood oxygen, are scattered and hard to connect quickly. As a result, a health problem such as fainting can start developing unnoticed until it becomes serious, and there is currently no simple system that runs onboard the spacecraft and warns the crew early without depending on Earth.

Our solution

ATHERA is an astronaut health-monitoring app that runs on the device itself, so it keeps working when the link to Earth is slow or completely lost. It tracks vital signs, spaceflight hazards such as radiation and blood biomarkers, and mental well-being in one place, and an AI model combined with rule-based thresholds warns the crew early, before a problem becomes an emergency. Every value is clearly labelled as measured, derived, predicted or simulated, missing data is shown as missing rather than filled in, and the app never lets a simulated number pass as a real one. It also includes an offline companion for isolation support, an emergency workflow, and a crew view for mission control. The current prototype is built on simulated data alongside real NASA datasets, and is designed so real sensors and models can be plugged in later.
